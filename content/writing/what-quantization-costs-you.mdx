Every on-prem deployment I've done runs a quantized model, because quantization is what makes the hardware line in the budget survive contact with procurement. Serving a 70B-class model at FP16 wants roughly <R>140 GB</R> of memory for weights alone; at INT8 it wants roughly <R>70 GB</R>. That's the difference between one server and two, and it compounds through power, rack space, and failover.

The question is what you pay for that in quality. The literature answers with benchmarks; the honest answer is "it depends on your task," which is unhelpful until you measure your task. So here's what I've measured, with the caveats attached.

## The setup

The eval sets are the clients' own: document QA over their corpora, answers graded against reference answers, plus the retrieval-independent slices we use to separate model errors from retrieval errors. Same prompts, same retrieval, same decoding settings — the only variable is weight precision. This is the boring discipline that makes the numbers mean something: a quantization comparison on someone else's benchmark tells you about their distribution, not yours.

## What INT8 cost us

On document QA — extract, summarize, answer with citations — the drop was consistently small: about <R>1 point</R> of graded accuracy on evals where the FP16 baseline scored in the high eighties to low nineties. For that workload, at that scale, it's a rounding error against a <R>~2×</R> memory saving. This is the case that pays for the whole approach, and it's also the most common enterprise workload by a wide margin.

Where it wasn't a rounding error:

- Multi-step numeric reasoning — chained calculations, financial reconciliation-style questions — degraded noticeably more, a few points rather than one. If your workload is arithmetic-shaped, quantize more carefully or not at all.
- Low-resource-language output got subtly worse in fluency before it got worse in correctness. Grading only on accuracy would have missed it; a client's bilingual reviewers didn't.
- The tails moved more than the means. Worst-case outputs got somewhat stranger, which matters if your risk profile is set by the worst answer, not the average one.

## The method is the takeaway

I'd hold the specific numbers loosely — they're two corpora, a handful of model families, one year. The transferable part is the procedure: hold everything constant except precision, grade on your own eval set, and read the slices, not just the headline number. It's a day or two of work, and it converts "quantization is basically fine" from folklore into a measurement with your name on it.

The reason this matters beyond engineering hygiene: quantization is a load-bearing term in the on-prem cost argument. The <R>~2×</R> saving is real, but it's only honest if you've priced the quality you traded for it — on your task, not on a benchmark.
